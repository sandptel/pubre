Chapter 1. The Rain

The rain had come in off the sea before dawn, and by the time Ada Marsh climbed the tower it was falling straight and grey and patient, the way rain falls when it means to stay all day. It ran down the lantern glass in long threads. It drummed on the gallery rail, filled the gutters, and found the one loose slate over the oil store, as it did every spring.

She trimmed the wicks, wiped the lenses, and stood a while watching the wet light come up over the headland. Below her the path to the landing was a stream; the puddles in the yard had joined hands. Somewhere out in the drizzle a gull complained, and then thought better of it.

At nine the supply boat came round the point, low in the water, its skipper hunched under an oilskin that shone like a seal. Tobias Reed brought her in against the landing with the ease of a man who had done it in worse rain than this, and threw Ada the line before she had reached the bottom step.

"You'll catch your death, standing about in this," he said.

"I've stood in worse."

"Not without a hat."

"I had a hat. The wind had opinions about it."

He laughed, and the rain ran off the brim of his own hat in a little waterfall. "Paraffin, flour, two sacks of coal, the post--and a parcel from your sister, if the damp hasn't got into it."

"If the damp had got into it, Tobias, you'd have said so first."

"I would," he admitted. "I'm a terrible liar in wet weather."

They carried the coal up between them, the sacks dark and dripping, their boots sliding on the wet stones. In the kitchen Ada set the kettle on and hung his oilskin by the range, where it steamed and smelled of tar and salt.

"Mrs. Penhale says there's a dance at the inn on Saturday," Tobias said, warming his hands. "She asked me to ask you. Mr. Penhale says you won't come."

"Mr. Penhale is usually right."

"He's never right. That's why she married him--so there'd be someone in the house to be wrong." He paused. "Will you come?"

"In this rain?"

"It'll clear by Saturday."

"You don't know that."

"No," he said, "but I'd like it to be true, which is nearly the same thing..."

Outside, the rain went on, soft and steady on the roof, and neither of them said anything for a while. The kettle began to sing. Down at the landing the boat rocked against its fenders, and the drops on the window ran together and apart, together and apart, like people who could not quite decide.

* * *

When he had gone, Ada opened her sister's parcel at the table. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth against exactly this weather, were a pair of gloves, a jar of damson jam, and a note that said only, Wear these. Do not argue. She put the gloves on, and did not argue, and went up to light the lamp an hour early because the afternoon had grown so dark with rain.
