Chapter 4. A Night at the Inn

It was night when they reached the village, a clear cold night with the moon nearly full and the stars so thick over the harbour that the sky looked salted. Tobias had brought the boat across in the last of the light; now the lamps of the inn were yellow squares in the dark, and the sound of a fiddle came down the street to meet them.

Mrs. Penhale met them at the door, broad and pink and smelling of cloves. "Ada Marsh! I said you'd come. Didn't I say she'd come?"

"You said she wouldn't," said her husband from behind the bar.

"I said nothing of the kind, Mr. Penhale."

"You said it twice," replied Mr. Penhale. "Once on Tuesday and once at breakfast."

"Pay no attention to him," said his wife. "Come in, come in, you're letting the night in."

Inside it was all candlelight and noise. Someone had hung paper lanterns from the beams; they swung a little whenever the door opened, and the shadows swung with them. The fiddler was playing something fast and the floor was full, boots and skirts and elbows, and the windows had steamed up so that the stars outside were only a blur.

"Will you dance?" Tobias asked.

"I don't dance."

"Everyone dances at the Penhales'. It's in the lease."

"Then I'll sit by the fire and watch everyone keep the lease."

"I'll sit with you," he said.

"You'll want to dance."

"I'll want to sit with you more."

She looked at him then, properly, in the candlelight, and found that she had nothing clever to say. So she let him take her hand, and they danced, badly, to the end of the tune, and then to the end of the next one.

Later, walking back to the harbour under the moon, they did not talk much. The tide was out and the boats lay tilted on the mud, silver in the moonlight. Above them the stars went on and on, more than anyone had ever counted, and somewhere behind them the fiddle started up again, small and far away in the night.
